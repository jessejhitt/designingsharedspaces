A trial toolkit for activating underused religious infrastructure in London, capturing urban design examples to create more shared community spaces.

Why do we need a creative database of case studies?

London As a post secular city

Different forms of public life are emerging.

Religious identity is diversifying and declining in the same neighbourhoods simultaneously.

Religious spaces continue to be parts of community life despite shifts to “secular” planning.

Which has led to planning that is not neutral.

It can ignore religious stakeholders, and treat religious spaces as nuisances when they have so much potential — or are already contributing to their communities, but are perhaps not public.

Why adaptable spaces?

Because they are already there. Naves, halls, undercrofts, churchyards and cemeteries are large, well located, structurally sound and — thanks to heritage protection — likely to outlast whatever is built next to them. Adapting one is almost always cheaper, greener and faster than building new shared space from nothing.

Why does design matter?

Because openness is a spatial condition, not a policy. A step, a closed door, an unlit path or an unclear sign will keep people out no matter how welcoming the intention. The interventions in this database show that the moves that produce interaction are often small — seating, thresholds, timetables, a café counter — and that they can be designed deliberately.

Why a database?

  • Called for at the inter-faith planning policy forum.
  • This is specific, but expandable to other ways religion has adapted.
  • Focusing on design and planning — where the existing toolkits stop, because they are written for project planning rather than for spatial decisions.

Starting with churches and churchyards: around 20-25 churches close a year in the UK, many of them large, well located, and conserved through heritage.

Contributions to practice, and where it goes next.

  • More sites, more people involved.
  • To active sites too, not only troubled ones.
  • So that charities, faith organisations and grant-makers can look for opportunities to fund projects in opportunity areas.
  • To increase interaction — connection — through design.
  • To a fairer and more shared conversation about religion in public space: religion is not disappearing, it is adapting, fluctuating, changing.

The post office in the nave

When West Hampstead's post office closed, St James' Church took it in — along with a café, a soft play, and eventually a debt advice service and community fridge, all funded by the tills. Worship never left; it just learned to share.

The Sherriff Centre is the database's benchmark for social-enterprise publicness: everyday services as the front door to a sacred room.
